We document how climate shocks drove migration in Brazil through the 20th century and build a structural model accounting for migration and congestion costs and agglomeration gains to study different policy reactions to such shocks. Under the model, subsidizing migration from climate-disadvantaged regions is welfare increasing for migrants and for non migrant residentes of destination regions, but is welfare decreasing for non migrant residents of origin regions; small subsidies have positive aggregate welfare effect. Underlying these findings there are significant heterogeneities: residents of the worse-affected regions lose as much as B% welfare, while residents of most-benfitted regions gain as much as G%. The results shed light on the tradeoffs surrounding leveraging migration as a climate adaptation technology
